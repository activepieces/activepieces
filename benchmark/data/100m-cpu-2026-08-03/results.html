What 100m-CPU workers can and can't do

A customer running 100m workers saw 1–2 s medians and frequent errors, and asked whether our benchmark reflects real flows — theirs mostly wait on external APIs. We ran two benchmarks on the same fleet: the standard CPU-bound flow, and an I/O-bound variant with a 5-second external call.

The orchestration overhead is a roughly constant ~0.6–0.7 s — it does not scale with the flow's external latency. The benchmark flow is that overhead and nothing else, which is why it reads as "high CPU impact": it is a CPU stress test by construction.

Summary

What this means for running at 100m

The cold path is where 100m hurts

Cold operation@ 500m (reference)@ 100m (measured)Status
First request — engine fork + boot, CPU-bound flow ~2.0 s 9.3 s completed
First request — cold cache, flow with HTTP piece — 16.8 s completed
Flow publish with a cold, heavier piece (40-worker pass) never observed exceeded safety timeout failed, retry ok
This is the likely source of the customer's instability
Control-plane engine operations (publish, enable, test step) and fresh engine forks share the worker's CPU cap. At 100m they take 5× longer, so under churn — deploys, scale-up, cache eviction, AP_REUSE_SANDBOX=false — they collide with sync-webhook budgets and safety timeouts and surface as errors. Steady-state traffic was error-free.

Benchmark ≠ real workload

The standard flow is pure serialization + orchestration — a CPU stress test. For API-waiting flows, read it as the fixed overhead (~0.6–0.7 s warm at 100m), not the total.

100m holds — if it stays warm

Zero errors across 2000 measured requests. The risk at 100m is entirely in cold work: 9–17 s first-request boots and control-plane ops that can hit safety timeouts.

Slots, not CPU, set the cost

With concurrency-1 workers, an I/O-bound fleet is limited by slot count while CPU idles at ~23%. Raising in-flight flows per worker is the lever behind a 5× cost reduction.
